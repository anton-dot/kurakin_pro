Data Mesh: Why Data May Need to Belong to Business Domains, Not One Data Team

Logistics and customer service mean different things by a fulfilled order. Who owns each fact, who defines the metric, and what should a shared data platform provide?

In an executive report, one team counts an order as “fulfilled” when it leaves the warehouse. Another counts it only after the customer accepts delivery. The analytics team can join both datasets, but it cannot invent the company's bonus policy or decide on behalf of the business which event counts.

These disagreements often end up in a central data team's backlog. That team can build pipelines and analytical models, but it gradually becomes a translator between every business unit. Data Mesh is a response to this ownership problem, not a requirement to replace one warehouse with several new technologies.

Owning a fact is different from owning the server

Logistics can own the fact that a consignment was handed to a carrier. Customer service or fulfillment can own evidence that delivery was accepted. A company-wide metric of completed orders needs a separate decision about its definition, purpose and exceptions.

Data Mesh is commonly described through four connected principles: domain-oriented ownership, data as a product, self-service data infrastructure and federated computational governance. They are set out in Zhamak Dehghani's original explanation. None requires a dedicated data lake for every department.

A domain is not automatically a box on an organization chart. Some business facts cross existing team boundaries and need an agreed owner. Assigning ownership in a catalog without giving that owner authority, skills and time merely renames the central backlog.

A dataset becomes a product when someone can use it reliably

Consider a data product called “carrier handovers.” Consumers need more than column names. They need a definition of handover, identifiers that survive corrections, data freshness, known exclusions, support contacts and a policy for breaking changes. Can this dataset support financial month-end reporting, or is it only suitable for a live operational dashboard?

Test the product with a real consumer. The team calculating bonuses asks for completed orders; the publishing team explains what its feed can and cannot establish. They should examine canceled handovers, partial deliveries and corrected timestamps. Those examples expose more than a slide about product ownership.

Putting a collection of extracts into a catalog does not automatically create a data product. Without a defined consumer, quality expectations and a lifecycle, decentralized ownership creates more places to look for an answer.

Decentralized ownership still needs a common platform

If every domain independently builds storage, access control, lineage, monitoring and publishing, the result is an expensive collection of incompatible systems. A self-service platform should provide repeatable ways to ingest, discover, secure, observe and version data.

The platform does not need to know what “fulfilled order” means. It needs to let the domain publish the accepted definition reliably. Similarly, security teams can enforce organization-wide rules for sensitive information without taking over the domain's business vocabulary.

A familiar failure mode is to declare product teams responsible for their datasets while requiring a central platform ticket for every schema change, data check or access request. The coordination cost has not fallen. Before assigning more responsibility, check whether teams can actually fulfill it with the tools and support available.

Shared governance does not require one universal meaning

Common governance is not a company-wide dictionary that flattens every distinction. Teams can agree how to identify organizations, classify sensitivity, document datasets and test quality while allowing sales and logistics to use the word “order” for different business purposes.

Derived datasets need particular care. Joining logistics records to customer details may create a more sensitive product than either source on its own. Its owner needs legitimate permission to combine the data, an access policy, a retention limit and identified consumers. Domain ownership is not permission to bypass company controls.

Cross-domain metrics require agreements between the owners of underlying facts. Logistics may publish handover events, fulfillment may publish delivery acceptance, and a separate curated model can calculate completed orders for the agreed bonus policy. The right to calculate that metric should not be confused with the right to rewrite its underlying events.

Published data creates continuing obligations

A dataset with consumers now has versions, expectations and support costs. A new interpretation of a status should not silently rewrite historical reports. Changes require an impact assessment, a period of compatibility and sometimes explicit recalculation. Quality checks and access reviews consume real capacity in the domain team.

That capacity matters. A team already struggling to operate its core product cannot simply be assigned a second full data-engineering practice. Start with one or two valuable datasets where semantic confusion creates recurring, expensive delays.

For our example, success is not the count of registered “data products.” It is the ability to reproduce the completed-orders metric, identify the people responsible for its facts, explain the distinction between shipping and acceptance, and change the definition safely. If a central data team already achieves this with little friction, reorganizing around Data Mesh may cost more than it saves.
