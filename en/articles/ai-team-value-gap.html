Why AI Isn't Delivering Value to Teams

A task can become eight times faster with AI while the customer sees only a small improvement. The missing value is usually in the process around the tool, not the model itself.

English translation of the original Russian article First published on E-xecutive, October 9, 2026 ↗

Generative AI is already part of everyday work in many companies. But there is a substantial difference between “we have introduced AI tools” and “we have achieved something useful.” How can you tell whether you are paying for another subscription or actually improving the business?

How AI helps us avoid work that never needed doing

Imagine a fairly ordinary meeting. The commercial director wants a “Prepare Proposal” button. A salesperson clicks it, gets a document, and sends it to the client. “What is so difficult about that?” he asks.

Forty minutes later, he knows about the company's complicated architecture, legacy constraints, and an engineering team already burdened by technical debt. The button has not appeared, but there is now a much better understanding of why it does not exist.

The engineers have a point. Prices live in one system, discounts in another, stock updates arrive late, and special terms for important clients are kept mainly in the memory of Sergey, the account manager. Sergey is on vacation, so a portion of the corporate knowledge base is temporarily unavailable. All this information must be assembled, reconciled, and verified before anyone can produce a correct proposal.

That evening, the director opens an AI tool and describes the same idea, adding a screenshot of the existing application. The model produces a mock-up. He asks it to rearrange the fields, compare options, then remove half of what it just added. Eventually he realizes that what he needs is not really a button at all, but a small assistant for the sales team.

Nobody sighs into a microphone, rolls their eyes over a carefully sourced oat-milk latte, or reminds him that yesterday's requirements were different.

It would be easy to declare a victory for AI over slow software development. But that would miss the point. The engineers were explaining how to build a working system. The model helped the director see what that system might look like. He could try an idea, look at it, and clarify what he wanted without setting a small production process in motion.

This is where the business starts to appreciate AI. Sometimes a manager needs to see an idea before they can explain it convincingly. Not every half-formed thought deserves a project, but neither should every thought have to compete for a place on the development backlog.

Consider another familiar scene. A team works on a solution all week. On Friday, the stakeholder takes one look and says, “Now I understand what I really need.” You can usually read the team's enthusiasm for this intellectual breakthrough on their faces. Officially, the requirements have changed. In practice, work must be redone, deadlines moved, and somebody has to explain why this could not have been established on Monday.

That is why discovery, prototyping, and agreement on requirements have an economic purpose: they keep expensive work from starting before there is enough clarity. But sometimes the customer genuinely cannot describe the solution in advance. They need to see one possibility to understand why it is wrong. “Show me something and I'll tell you what I don't like” may annoy the people doing the work, but it is often an accurate description of how an idea takes shape.

Generative tools let us do part of that exploration before committing to implementation. We can compare mock-ups, test scenarios, draft calculations, or discover that an idea is not worth pursuing at all. It is much easier to throw away a failed option before a lot of time has been spent on it and before somebody has announced it as a strategic initiative.

In that sense, one of AI's most valuable contributions is making it cheaper to discover what is worth doing. When the first attempt is easy to discard, the customer can admit they were wrong and the team has less reason to defend something simply because it has already been built.

There is one important boundary, though. Changing your mind about a mock-up and changing the terms of thousands of contracts are very different costs. AI can make testing an idea cheaper. It does not automatically make the consequences of acting on that idea cheap.

Why an AI subscription does not replace professional expertise

Small companies regularly need expertise that does not justify a full-time position. Today they need to investigate some data, tomorrow to analyze sales, and next week to build a small internal tool. There is not enough work to employ a specialist continuously, but without the work something keeps getting stuck.

Hiring a contractor by the hour is one option. But you still have to find the person, explain the task, and transfer the context. “It's literally half an hour of work,” says the customer. Two meetings later, everyone finally agrees where to look, only to discover that they need a different specialist. Nobody has been idle, but that half-hour has become rather expensive.

AI can handle part of this work without a separate hire: produce an initial analysis, prepare a draft, and test a few assumptions. Access to some capabilities becomes more flexible. You might need a tool all day today, for twenty minutes tomorrow, and not at all the day after. You do not have to invent a new job description because last week's project ended.

Of course, the cost of reviewing the results and maintaining whatever you build may not be quite so flexible.

It is tempting to compare a specialist's salary with a subscription fee and rush off to approve the savings. I would first calculate who will verify the AI's output, correct its errors, connect the data sources, and maintain the solution. A subscription does not include professional accountability. The person providing that accountability normally expects to be paid, too. The financial model becomes less festive, but more useful.

The opportunity for leaders is to combine a permanent team with on-demand tools differently. People can concentrate on work requiring business knowledge, independent judgment, and responsibility, while some preparatory tasks become cheaper. But first you need to understand what actually makes up the work. An organization chart alone tells you very little about which tasks can be delegated to a tool and which depend on someone's experience.

How a faster technology can make a process slower

Take a hypothetical sales proposal. Writing the document takes four hours, while collecting information and waiting for approvals takes another thirty-six. The whole cycle is forty hours.

Suppose AI reduces drafting time to thirty minutes, with everything else unchanged. Has the process become eight times faster? No. The customer now receives the proposal after 36.5 hours instead of 40. The complete cycle has improved by about 9%.

That is still worthwhile. The customer, however, is unlikely to share your excitement. They were waiting for a proposal, not watching your document-generation benchmark.

To achieve a bigger improvement, the company has to look at those remaining hours:

  • Why does the salesperson have to wait for an up-to-date price list?
  • Why must the director approve every standard discount?
  • Which checks could happen in parallel?
  • Why are people asking a colleague for information that the colleague simply retrieves from a system everyone can access?

Buying new technology does not solve problems that require organizational change. Faster work at one stage may even slow down the next. If analysts previously prepared five reports a week and can now produce twenty, their manager does not suddenly gain the capacity to make four times as many decisions. They just have a bigger unread pile.

We could add another AI to summarize the output of the first, then a third to extract the highlights from those summaries. At some point, it might be worth asking what decision we were trying to make in the first place.

That is why I separate two questions: What became cheaper to produce? And what can the company now do because of it?

If proposals reach customers sooner and fewer deals are lost because of delays, the connection is clear. If documents are produced faster but sit in the same approval queue, the tool may already be useful, but the business benefit remains to be realized. The bottleneck has moved, and the next improvement has to happen there.

Why we need people willing to doubt AI's answers

Imagine a manager planning a new service and asking an AI model to write the business case. The result is neat: target audience, benefits, launch stages, expected outcomes. “Yes, that's pretty much what I meant,” the manager thinks. With every paragraph, their confidence grows.

This is when you need a person prepared to spoil the mood by asking some awkward questions:

  • Why do you believe customers need this?
  • Who will deliver what you are promising?
  • What will happen to existing service commitments?
  • Could we solve the problem without a new product, department, or IT system?

That specialist is less pleasant to deal with than the model. But they help prevent a convincing proposal from being mistaken for a tested hypothesis. Assumption-checking needs to be an explicit part of the process, with clear criteria. It cannot rely on the hope that somebody will independently discover everything the requester forgot to mention.

The easier it becomes to generate persuasive recommendations, the more important it is to verify them. The reviewer needs expertise, time, and the authority to stop a launch. If someone hands over fifty pages ten minutes before submission and says, “Just check that everything is fine,” human oversight mostly exists in the policy document. In reality, the company has merely decided whose name will appear beneath the result.

How to make AI produce an actual business result

Start with one repeatable process and a few specific decisions. This may be less impressive than a clever demo prompt, but you can understand what the company intends to achieve and who will have to change their work to achieve it.

Let's stay with the sales proposal example. There is a customer, a deadline, errors, and money involved. That gives us something more meaningful to improve than the quality of a demonstration.

1. Map the process as a queue of work

Follow the path from a customer's request to a completed, approved proposal and inspect a few recent cases. How much time was spent working, and how much waiting? Where did documents come back for revision? Which mistakes had to be corrected? “We're usually pretty fast” is not a useful answer until everyone agrees how to measure “usually.”

The output should be a simple map showing working time and waiting time at each stage. A proposal that took two hours and one that took ten days may have followed the same formal workflow. Find out what was different in the second case. Were data missing? Did special terms need approval? Or did an email simply get lost between participants? These problems need different solutions, even if they all disappear into the same attractive average.

If waiting for prices causes the longest delay, begin with prices. It is better to find the weak point than to automate the wrong step and then work heroically to justify the choice.

2. Make sure the work is worth accelerating

Before automating a step, ask what would happen if you stopped doing it altogether. Perhaps a weekly report has been prepared and distributed carefully for years, but when someone asks, “Which decision did we make using it last time?”, the room becomes noticeably quiet. Maybe the report should be less frequent, sent to someone else, or presented differently. That conversation should happen before improving the report-generation prompt.

In our example, the company might approve a standard range of commercial terms in advance and escalate only exceptions. AI can help prepare the document, while a change in decision rights removes the need to wait for the director's signature on every ordinary deal. A substantial part of the improvement comes from the management decision, not a better model prompt.

But first ask why the restriction exists. Some checks genuinely protect money, customers, or system reliability. Others are habits whose original purpose nobody remembers. Neither the age of a rule nor employees' irritation proves whether it is useful. The answer needs investigation, not another campaign to “eliminate all bureaucracy.”

3. Give someone ownership of the result—and authority to act

A project needs someone accountable for the entire chosen process, not merely for connecting the new tool. In the proposal example, that person owns the turnaround time and quality of the completed proposal. They must be able to negotiate changes with sales, finance, and IT.

Define which decisions they can make independently, which must be escalated, and who resolves conflicting priorities. “Work it out between yourselves” sounds like delegation until the parties have incompatible objectives and neither can change them. If everyone can reject a change but nobody owns the total delay, the new technology will simply receive detailed instructions for supporting the old process.

Agree on what happens after the pilot, too. Who updates the rules, watches quality, handles exceptions, and pays for support? Name people and allocate time, not just departments. “We'll figure that out after launch” means the launch has been planned more thoroughly than the work that follows it. The contractor may deliver, the project group may disband, but customer requests will, inconveniently, continue.

4. Decide how much autonomy the system should have

Drafting an email, sending it to a customer, and changing the terms of a deal are different levels of authority. They should not be treated as interchangeable tasks. Before the pilot, define what data the tool can access, which sources are authoritative, and which actions it may perform on its own. That is easier than having the conversation after a customer happily accepts terms the company never intended to offer.

Prices and discounts must come from approved sources. Calculations should be checked separately from the quality of the prose. At first, sending the final proposal might remain a human action. I would increase the system's autonomy based on test results, not on how confidently it presents its answers. Critical actions need logs and a clear way to stop or reverse an operation.

You also have to assemble the context: current rules, exceptions, constraints, and owners of disputed cases. And clean up the information that the process depends on. If a folder contains “Price_Final,” “Price_New,” and “Use_This_One,” the model should not have to guess which file survived the last meeting. Neither should a human.

5. Decide what the saved hours will become

Before launching, agree what the reduction in work should achieve:

  • Will salespeople handle more requests?
  • Will the team absorb growth without additional hiring?
  • Will overtime, contractor spending, or lost deals decrease?

These are different objectives. “Our employees will become more productive” is pleasant to say, but difficult to verify.

Time saved should not automatically be booked as a reduction in cost. Twenty minutes saved by each of thirty employees adds up to ten hours, but it does not magically produce one employee who is free for ten hours. You may need to redistribute tasks before the company can use that capacity. Otherwise the savings exist in a report while the person you hoped would take on additional work is still fully occupied.

Talk to employees in advance as well. Starting a technology rollout by announcing how many people will soon be unnecessary is unlikely to generate enthusiasm. That does not mean promising that jobs and responsibilities will never change. It means explaining which tasks may disappear, which skills will matter next, and how success will be evaluated.

6. Allow the pilot to fail

Define success criteria before the experiment. For example, the end-to-end time to handle a request should fall without unacceptable quality loss. The cost of an accepted result should include model usage, review, corrections, integrations, and support. Set these rules before showing executives a slide with a victory already drawn on it. After that, the discussion can shift quietly from “What did we learn?” to “What should we call this so it sounds like success?”

Test ordinary work, including the awkward cases. Three carefully chosen successes mainly demonstrate your ability to choose three good examples. If an experienced employee corrects AI output every evening, count that work. “It's fully automated; Marina just checks it a little afterwards” becomes a particularly interesting statement when you ask Marina how long “a little” actually takes.

The pilot should be allowed to end in one of three decisions: scale it, change the approach, or stop. An experiment that cannot be stopped because of public commitments is no longer an experiment. The organization is now defending a previous decision instead of evaluating a new tool.

If review effort consumes all the time saved, that is a useful finding. You can narrow the use case, redesign the process, or decide not to deploy it. Part of the pilot's cost was the price of learning this before scaling. Finding out after the whole company has been connected and the celebration has been scheduled is considerably worse.

Conclusion

AI can help us experiment faster, abandon bad ideas more cheaply, and complete some work without a dedicated specialist. Those are real benefits.

But the technology will not decide which steps are no longer necessary, who should have authority to make decisions, or how saved time should be used. That remains management's job.

Those decisions determine whether AI-driven speed becomes a nice demonstration—or a measurable change in business results.
