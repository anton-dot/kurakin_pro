How to Tell When a Manager Becomes the Team Bottleneck

Sometimes a team is slow not because the people are weak or the process is bad. Everyone is simply waiting for one person: the manager.

Managers usually become bottlenecks not because they want to slow the team down. Usually the opposite: they try to help everywhere and gradually make themselves mandatory for every decision.

The problem becomes visible when team speed is determined not by the number of people or complexity of work, but by the manager's free time.

Everyone is waiting for your decision

If work regularly stops with “we need the manager's approval,” ask whether those decisions really require your level.

When the same questions keep moving upward, the problem is often unclear decision boundaries rather than weak discipline.

Your calendar becomes a queue

Another sign is that people cannot move until they get time with you.

In that system, the manager's calendar becomes a request-processing queue. The bigger the team becomes, the worse the model scales.

You constantly redo other people's work

If you delegate and then usually rewrite the document, change the decision, or rebuild the result, the task was delegated but ownership was not.

The team quickly learns that the final version will be yours anyway and stops investing in independence.

Everything slows down without you

A manager's vacation is a useful system test. If hiring, releases, and key decisions stop for a week, the issue is not that the person is irreplaceable. The management system is fragile.

A strong manager should make their absence less visible over time, not more dangerous.

People bring problems without options

When the manager always knows the answer, the team learns to bring questions instead of recommendations.

Return ownership with questions such as: “What options do you see?”, “What do you recommend?”, and “Which risk matters most?”

Delegation is not task distribution

Real delegation means defining decision boundaries: what the person can decide alone, what they should inform you about, and what genuinely requires approval.

Without this, employees either ask permission for everything or make decisions that the manager later reverses.

Start with repeating decisions

You do not need to delegate everything at once. Look at questions that reach you every week and move them downward together with the context and criteria.

If the team cannot move faster than your calendar, the answer is not to work even more. It is to build a system in which fewer things require you personally.
