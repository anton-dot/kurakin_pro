Does Your Company Need a CTO?

A company does not need a CTO simply because it has IT. It needs one when technology becomes part of business decisions and someone must connect those decisions over the long term.

Sometimes a CTO appears too early because “a technology company should have a CTO.” Sometimes the role arrives too late, after software has already become critical to the business but technology decisions are still being made in fragments.

I would start not with the title, but with a simpler question: what problem is this person supposed to solve?

Having Developers Does Not Automatically Mean You Need a CTO

If a small team is building one product, architectural decisions are clear, and the founder works well with a strong technical lead, a separate executive role may add little value.

A title does not create a new management layer by itself. Sometimes a company hires a CTO and effectively gets an expensive Tech Lead with a larger title.

You Need a CTO When Technology Decisions Become Business Decisions

Architecture starts affecting product speed. Technical debt affects the cost of change. Reliability affects customer relationships. Technology choices affect the ability to hire and develop the engineering team.

At that point the business needs someone who can discuss technology not as a collection of tools, but as a system of constraints and opportunities.

When the CEO Becomes the Translator Between Business and IT

One sign of missing technology leadership is that every difficult conflict between business and engineering rises to the CEO.

Business says IT is too slow. IT says requirements keep changing. Teams argue about priorities, deadlines, and quality, and the final referee becomes someone who should not be resolving every technical disagreement personally.

A CTO in this situation is not there to “defend IT.” The job is to make technology tradeoffs understandable to the business and business priorities understandable to engineering.

The CTO Role Is Not Only About Technology

As a company grows, many technology problems become organizational problems. How should teams be structured? Which expertise should be centralized? Who owns quality? How do you avoid turning architecture into an endless committee?

If the CTO only chooses technologies, a large part of the role remains empty.

Do Not Hire a CTO If You Are Not Ready to Give Them Decisions

There is little point in hiring a strong technology executive if every meaningful decision will still be made by the founder or CEO.

Responsibility without authority quickly turns a CTO into a consultant inside their own company.

Before hiring, define which decisions this person can make independently, what outcomes they own, and where the boundaries between technology, product, and business sit.

Sometimes You Need Something Else

If the main problem is engineering discipline, perhaps you need a strong engineering manager. If one team needs technical direction, perhaps you need a Tech Lead. If the challenge is stable operation of internal corporate systems, CTO may not be the most useful answer either.

Do not treat organizational complexity by adding a title.

A Simple Test

I would seriously consider a CTO when technology already influences strategy, the company regularly makes expensive long-term technical decisions, the engineering organization no longer fits inside one technical leader’s head, and the CEO increasingly has to resolve business-versus-IT conflicts personally.

At that point the CTO is no longer decoration on an org chart. The role exists to connect technology, people, and business into one manageable system.

A company needs a CTO not because it has a lot of technology, but because the cost of disconnected technology decisions has become too high.
