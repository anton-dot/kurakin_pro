Outbox Pattern: How Not to Lose an Event Between the Database and the Queue

A system can save an order successfully and fail a millisecond before publishing the event. One part of the business knows the order exists; another behaves as if it never happened. The Outbox Pattern closes that gap.

Event-driven systems often need to do two things together: change business data and tell other systems about it.

For example, create an order in the database and publish an OrderCreated event. Those are still two separate operations.

If the database succeeds and the broker is unavailable, the business fact exists but the event is missing.

What problem are we solving?

The Outbox Pattern stores the business change and a record of the future event in the same local database transaction.

A separate process reads the outbox and publishes the event later. Failed publication can be retried.

What does the business gain?

The main benefit is fewer invisible inconsistencies between systems.

An order, payment, or document action should not disappear from the next business step just because infrastructure failed for a moment.

That means fewer manual reconciliations and fewer cases where different teams see different realities.

What does the team gain?

The team gets a practical way to connect a local database transaction with asynchronous event publication without a distributed transaction.

In return, delivery is usually at least once, so consumers must tolerate duplicates. The team must monitor stuck records, publication delay, and cleanup.

What does the customer gain?

The customer sees fewer strange states: an order exists but no notification follows, a payment succeeds but the next process never starts, or a signed document does not move forward.

What do we pay for it?

The price is extra machinery and a small delay between the local change and event publication.

There is now an outbox table, a delivery process, monitoring, retry logic, and duplicate handling.

When is the Outbox Pattern unnecessary?

If the system does not publish important events after data changes, or losing an occasional notification is acceptable, the pattern may be unnecessary.

Questions to ask before deciding

  • What happens if data is saved but the event is not published?
  • Can consumers safely handle duplicates?
  • How quickly must events reach downstream systems?
  • How will we detect stuck records?
  • Who owns outbox retention and cleanup?

In the end

The Outbox Pattern is useful when data changes and the events that represent them need to survive failures together.

Its business value is preventing an important process from disappearing in the tiny technical gap between a database and a message broker.
