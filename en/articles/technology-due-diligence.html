Technology Due Diligence: What to Check Before Acquiring a Company

When acquiring a technology company, the buyer does not only acquire a product and revenue. It also acquires architecture, people, dependencies, and accumulated decisions whose cost may appear after the deal.

Technology due diligence is sometimes treated as a code quality review. But clean code alone does not make a company a good acquisition, and an old stack does not automatically make it a bad one.

The more useful question is: which technology obligations, constraints, and risks transfer to the buyer with the business?

Start with the connection between technology and the business

Understand which parts of the technology are actually critical to revenue, customers, and future growth.

What cannot be replaced quickly? Which capabilities differentiate the product? Where does the company rely on manual work? What happens if load, customer count, or geography changes significantly?

Without this context, architecture review easily turns into a debate about preferences.

Review architectural constraints, not architectural beauty

Every working system contains tradeoffs. What matters is where those tradeoffs are already costing the business speed, money, or options.

Which components are difficult to change? Where are dependencies too tight? What fails repeatedly? Which changes does the team avoid? Are there parts of the product that cannot evolve without a major project?

Those are the real technology obligations the future owner is acquiring.

Understand who the system depends on

One of the most expensive risks may sit in people rather than code.

Is there one engineer who understands a critical system? Who knows how to release? Who understands the infrastructure? Who makes architecture decisions? What happens if two key people leave after the acquisition?

Documentation helps, but it does not always compensate for concentrated knowledge.

Understand the data

You need to know what data the company stores, where it comes from, who owns it, and how easily it can be restored or moved.

Risk becomes especially serious when the product depends heavily on data but there is little clarity about quality, provenance, access, or recovery.

A data problem after the deal can be far more expensive than an unfashionable framework.

Review external dependencies

A product may look proprietary while much of its capability depends on vendors, licenses, cloud services, closed APIs, or a single contractor.

What happens if a vendor raises prices? Can the company switch? Which contract terms matter? Is there technology the company effectively does not control?

Vendor lock-in is not automatically a problem. It simply needs to be a conscious part of the deal economics.

Review security through consequences

Asking only whether a security policy exists tells you little. It is more useful to understand which scenarios can actually damage the business.

Who has production and data access? How are secrets managed? What happens if an account is compromised? Can systems be restored? Are critical changes made manually without controls?

The goal is not to find a company with no risk. No such company exists. The goal is to understand the size and manageability of the risk.

Estimate the cost of fixing issues, not just the number of issues

A long list of findings is nearly useless without priority. Ten minor architecture problems may cost less than one serious people dependency.

For each material constraint, ask whether it needs fixing at all, when it becomes critical, what organizational change it requires, and what happens if nothing is done.

Good technology due diligence does not answer “how good is the code?” It helps the buyer understand the technology reality they are actually acquiring and how much freedom it leaves the business after the deal.
