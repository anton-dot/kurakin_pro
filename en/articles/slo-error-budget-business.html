SLO and Error Budgets: When Should Releases Slow Down?

An uptime chart can be green while customers cannot pay. SLOs connect real customer outcomes with the choice between releases and reliability.

The business wants faster releases. Operations wants a break from releases because the last incident has left the service looking fragile. Without a shared definition of acceptable reliability, the argument goes nowhere. Everyone is defending a different kind of risk.

SLOs and error budgets are not just more numbers to put on a dashboard. They create an agreement about the customer experience the product must deliver, and give that agreement a role in decisions about change.

A healthy server can still mean a broken checkout

Imagine an online store. Its servers are up and the pages load, but customers cannot complete payment. Infrastructure monitoring may be green. The business still has a broken journey at the point where it matters most.

That is why I would begin with what the customer is trying to accomplish, not with server uptime. For checkout, successful completion and acceptable response time matter. For an internal report, the relevant question may be whether correct data is available when someone needs it to make a decision.

From that outcome, choose an SLI — a measurable service level indicator. It could be the share of checkout attempts that complete successfully. An SLO then states the target for that indicator over an agreed measurement window. This is a testable expectation, not a promise that nothing will ever fail.

An error budget is not a licence to break things

If the target allows less than perfect operation, the difference represents some tolerated share of unsuccessful outcomes. That is the error budget.

The point is not to decide how many customers we are allowed to disappoint. It is to make risk a conscious decision instead of a negotiation driven by the last outage. While the service is meeting its objective, changes can continue, with the team watching how quickly that allowance is being consumed.

Different measures need different budgets. A request-based objective produces a budget of unsuccessful requests, not a number of downtime minutes. You must decide what counts as an attempt, what counts as failure, which time period applies, and which users are in scope.

Metrics must describe the real customer experience. A checkout that hangs until the customer leaves is not successful merely because the application never recorded an error response.

When shipping less is the sensible business decision

Suppose the service has consumed most of its error budget. Another risky release could hurt customers more than its new feature would help them. Should it go out now? Perhaps not.

But a blanket ban on change is rarely the answer. An urgent security fix or a change that restores reliability may be necessary even after the budget is exhausted. So the policy should be agreed before the argument begins: which launches slow down, which fixes go ahead, and who can authorise exceptions.

A good policy is not a punishment for the team that had the incident. It gives that team permission to focus on reliability work without being asked to deliver the same feature roadmap at the same time.

There is another subtlety: a healthy-looking budget for an entire period can hide a sharp increase in failures right now. Waiting for the budget to hit zero is not good incident management. The speed of consumption matters too.

Reliability can be expensive in both directions

Checkout and a rarely used internal report should not automatically have identical targets. A failed payment can mean a lost sale. A short delay in a report may have no practical effect at all.

Higher reliability goals can require more redundancy, more engineering effort, more operational care and a slower appetite for change. This is not an argument for weak protection where failure is costly. It is an argument for matching the objective to the consequences.

And a target set too low has its own cost: it can make a poor experience look acceptable on paper. If customers keep struggling while the SLO stays green, the indicator may be wrong.

How I would introduce it

Start with one critical customer journey rather than launching a company-wide measurement programme. Product, engineering and operations should agree on four things:

  • What success looks like for the customer and where that outcome is measured.
  • Which quality objective and measurement window the business actually needs.
  • Who watches error-budget consumption and what triggers an early response.
  • Which releases pause after an SLO miss, and which corrective changes remain permitted.

If a third-party provider causes the failure, customers may still experience it as a failure of your product. Ownership of the customer outcome remains important, although the remedy may involve fallback design, escalation or a different provider arrangement.

An SLO will not make a service reliable by itself. It helps people make an adult decision about when to deliver more change and when to stop adding risk long enough to improve the service.

Google's example error-budget policy shows how to turn this agreement into operational rules. The useful part is the decision process, not copying somebody else's thresholds.
