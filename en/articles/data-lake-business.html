Data Lake: Why It Can Be Worth Keeping Data Before You Know How You Will Use It

Sensor readings, photos and repair reports might reveal failure patterns. Before keeping everything, the company needs a way to connect the records and govern access.

A maintenance company wants to understand whether equipment failures can be detected earlier. It has sensor readings, before-and-after repair photos, engineers' notes and records of replaced parts. The data lives in different formats, and there is no agreed analytical model for combining it yet.

A data lake can preserve those original datasets while the team works out how to analyse them. But “store everything and figure it out later” postpones the difficult decisions. Without identity, access controls and provenance, a year-old reading may no longer be traceable to the right machine, and a photograph may not be permissible to use.

Keep the source, but keep its context too

Every ingestion needs basic metadata: where the data came from, when a measurement actually occurred, when it arrived, which machine it belongs to and which format version it uses. Sensor readings also need units and, where relevant, calibration details. A value of 85 is not particularly useful if we cannot tell whether it means temperature, load percentage or a delayed duplicate sent after a network outage.

Original files should be preserved in a way that allows transformations to be repeated and audited. That does not mean every copy must live forever. It means controlled versions, integrity checks and a defined retention policy. Overwriting the source with an allegedly clean table removes the evidence needed to discover a bad conversion later.

Databricks' Medallion Architecture guide describes retaining raw records before progressively validating and preparing them. It is one organisational pattern, not a requirement to deploy three products or use a particular vendor.

From files to a question the business can answer

Failure analysis begins by linking readings to equipment and operating time. The team then needs to deduplicate messages, normalise timestamps and units, and distinguish a faulty sensor from genuine overheating. A suspicious record should remain traceable to its original form, since the cleaning rule itself may need correction.

Photographs present different problems. Which inspection do they belong to? Are they linked to a valid work order? Is the intended use permitted under the applicable agreements and privacy requirements? A blurry image can be stored correctly and still be unhelpful for training a model. Storage quality and analytical quality are not the same thing.

For management, collecting data and making a decision from data are two different milestones. Ingestion volume and metadata completeness show that collection works. A reproducible analysis on a defined set of equipment and failures is evidence that the material may support a particular business question.

A lake is not a shared folder without permissions

Raw datasets may contain personal information about staff and customers, property addresses, documents and commercially sensitive operating details. Exploratory analysis is not a reason to expose all of them to everyone.

Datasets need owners, permitted-use rules, access controls and audit trails. Researchers can often work with restricted or de-identified derivatives. Permission to use one such dataset should not automatically grant access to the source photos and documents.

The lifecycle also needs an ending. Why is a file being kept, when will its value be reviewed, when must it be deleted, and how will requests to correct or erase personal data be handled? Backup retention should not silently invalidate the company's stated deletion rules.

Cheap storage is not a cheap data platform

Cost extends beyond file capacity. Data has to be ingested, catalogued, processed, protected, located and sometimes transferred between systems. It is possible to pay for years of archives that nobody can query reliably. It is also possible to overbuild a sophisticated platform for one experiment.

I would begin with a bounded case: one equipment family, known failure types and sources whose provenance can actually be established. Measure how much material remains usable after quality checks, which hypotheses can be investigated and what the preparation costs.

If the business only needs recurring reports built from stable definitions, a data warehouse or a conventional reporting database may be enough. The lake becomes more compelling when heterogeneous source material is needed for several not-yet-fully-defined investigations and the company can support its ongoing governance.

What proves the first stage worked?

Take one repair event and reconstruct its path: original readings, related service report, image, cleaning rules and final analysis dataset. Another specialist should be able to obtain a comparable result without interviewing the author of every script.

Then test the awkward cases. A sensor was replaced, a device clock drifted, a file arrived late, or an employee requested an access restriction. If those cases have an explainable treatment, the retained data can support future questions. Otherwise another terabyte mainly increases the cost of the next investigation.
