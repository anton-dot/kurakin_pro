Strangler Pattern: Move Business Flows, Not the Entire Monolith

The legacy product is expensive to change, but a complete rewrite is an even bigger bet. How to migrate without paying for two platforms forever.

The company decides to rewrite its legacy system. The timeline looks convincing on the roadmap. Then reality intervenes: the existing product keeps evolving, new rules must be implemented twice, and the final cutover date moves further away. Eventually the replacement is chasing a target that never stops moving.

The Strangler Pattern replaces a running product incrementally. Business capabilities move to new components one at a time. But the key is not simply placing two applications behind a router. It is choosing boundaries where a transition can be completed and verified.

Move one complete business journey

Suppose an order system makes refund rules particularly difficult to change. Instead of migrating every screen and table, begin with that journey: identify its entry points, rules, data and external integrations.

Requests for the migrated flow gradually go to the new component. The rest still runs on legacy. Customers may not notice that two generations of technology are serving the product.

But if every action in the new service reaches into a dozen legacy tables, independence is largely an illusion. Ownership boundaries matter much more than the programming language used for the replacement.

Who owns the data during the transition?

Old and new applications often need access to shared information. Letting both update the same records without a clear protocol can create conflicting states and uncertainty about which result is authoritative.

Each migrated flow needs a source of truth, known synchronisation delays and a recovery path when communication fails. An Anti-Corruption Layer may help protect the new model from legacy assumptions.

Be especially suspicious of promises that traffic can always be switched back instantly. If the new application has written data that the old one cannot understand, flipping a routing flag may not be a safe rollback.

Make every step produce evidence

Large migrations often report the number of modules moved without showing that a business constraint has actually disappeared. I would measure whether the chosen flow is now easier to change, whether operating costs have changed and whether the legacy implementation can be shut down.

Define completion criteria and owners for retiring old paths before starting each slice. Without an exit condition, the temporary architecture can last for years and the business pays for both systems without getting the promised benefit.

When incremental migration is too much

A small, isolated component may be cheaper to replace with one controlled release. There is no benefit in building sophisticated routing for a simple, low-risk change.

Strangler is more useful when the product is business-critical, dependencies are poorly understood and the company cannot suspend development during a long rewrite.

A successful migration does not necessarily replace every last part of legacy. It ends when the business stops paying for the constraints that mattered enough to change.
