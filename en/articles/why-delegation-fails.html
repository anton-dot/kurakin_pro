Why Delegation Fails

A manager may say a task is delegated while keeping every meaningful decision. In that system, the employee receives responsibility without real authority.

Delegation failures are often blamed on the employee: they could not handle it, showed no initiative, or asked too many questions.

But quite often the system teaches exactly that behavior.

Responsibility moved, authority did not

If someone owns the outcome but cannot change priorities, negotiate with another team, or choose the method, delegation is incomplete.

It is responsibility without the tools to influence the result.

The manager takes the task back at the first problem

An employee hits a difficult point, the manager sees they can solve it faster, and takes the work back.

That may save time today. Over time, the team learns that difficult decisions will still be made by the boss.

Independent decisions are punished

If employees are criticized whenever they choose a path different from the one the manager would have chosen, they quickly stop choosing at all.

The next step is asking for approval on things they used to decide independently.

There is no right to do it differently

Some managers are willing to delegate only if the employee reproduces their exact method.

But when every decision is already determined, only the mechanical part of the work has been handed over.

Mistakes do not become learning

Without reviewing decisions, teams either repeat mistakes or learn to hide them.

Good delegation includes freedom to act and regular discussion of what worked, what did not, and why.

If every important decision still belongs to the manager after delegation, the problem is not the team's lack of independence. The team simply has nothing meaningful left to decide.
