Circuit Breaker: When to Stop Calling a Failing Service

A payment provider is slow. Waiting for it should not make the rest of the product slow as well. A circuit breaker puts a limit on how long the application keeps trying.

A payment provider stops answering promptly. It is not completely down, which makes the incident harder to spot. Requests sit there waiting for a timeout. Meanwhile, the application keeps sending new ones.

The obvious problem is that people cannot pay. The less obvious one is that waiting requests consume connections and workers. A failure in one external system can start affecting parts of the product that have nothing to do with payments.

A circuit breaker is a way to stop making that situation worse. Once a dependency is showing sustained signs of trouble, the application temporarily stops calling it. This is a deliberate failure instead of a slow, uncontrolled one.

Waiting is not neutral

Engineers are used to thinking of a request as successful or unsuccessful. But an unanswered request has a cost before it returns either result. It occupies some of the resources needed to serve the next request.

Under normal load, a few slow calls may not matter. When a dependency remains unhealthy, the waiting accumulates. Clients retry. The next layer may retry too. Eventually the original problem is only one part of the incident.

A timeout puts a maximum wait on an individual call. That is necessary, but it does not stop the next request from going to the same unhealthy dependency. And another timeout after another timeout is still a lot of wasted capacity.

The circuit breaker adds memory to the decision. It asks whether the recent pattern of failures makes another call worth attempting at all.

What actually happens when the circuit opens

Most implementations have three states. The names sound electrical, but the behaviour is straightforward.

Closed: calls go through as usual. The breaker observes failures and slow responses over a period of time. A single bad request should not normally be enough to declare an outage.

Open: the dependency has crossed the failure threshold. New calls are rejected immediately, without being sent to it. That protects local resources and gives the dependency room to recover.

Half-open: after a pause, a small number of trial calls are allowed through. If the dependency is healthy again, traffic can resume. If not, the circuit opens again.

The half-open state matters more than it first appears. Sending the entire backlog into a service that has just recovered can recreate the outage. Recovery needs a controlled return, not a starting pistol.

The awkward part is what the customer sees

Opening a circuit does not magically complete the business operation. It changes how the failure is presented and contained.

Suppose payments are unavailable but customers can still browse their accounts and review an order. Keeping those functions alive has value. Presenting a failed payment as a successful purchase does not.

The application may return a clear temporary error. Some workflows can put work into an explicit pending state; others cannot proceed until the provider is available. A read-only function may tolerate cached data, while a financial operation may require a much stricter response.

That distinction should be agreed with the product owner before an incident, not invented by an exception handler on the day.

I would rather have an honest message saying a payment could not be completed than a reassuring screen that leaves the customer wondering whether they have been charged.

Bad thresholds create a different kind of outage

There is no universal setting for how many failures should open the circuit or how long it should remain open. An internal service with heavy traffic and a rarely used third-party API have very different patterns.

Start by deciding which failures count. A timeout or a provider outage may indicate dependency health. A customer's invalid input or an ordinary business rejection usually does not. Treating every unsuccessful response as an infrastructure failure is an easy way to disable a healthy service.

The measurement window matters too. A high failure percentage over very few requests can be misleading. A threshold that is too slow to react offers little protection. One that is too sensitive creates needless unavailability.

Then decide what you will observe in production: circuit state changes, calls rejected while open, trial-call results, and the effect on customer-facing operations. A breaker that quietly stays open long after recovery is not a successful safety feature.

It is not a substitute for other limits

A breaker does not make the dependency faster, repair a database, or guarantee that the rest of the application has enough capacity.

For temporary failures, limited retries with backoff may help. But a retry loop should respect an open circuit, not fight it. If the problem is one workload consuming all available connections, resource isolation with a bulkhead solves a different part of the problem. If jobs keep arriving faster than anyone can process them, backpressure is the relevant control.

These mechanisms are often discussed together because incidents do not respect architecture diagrams. That does not mean every system needs all of them.

When I would leave it out

If the dependency is reliable enough for the workload, calls have clear deadlines, retries are bounded, and one failure cannot tie up shared resources, adding a circuit breaker may be more trouble than it is worth. It introduces states that have to be understood, tuned and tested.

Before adding one, I would answer three practical questions. Can this dependency make unrelated parts of the product unavailable? What exactly happens to the customer when we stop calling it? And how will we know it is safe to start again?

If those answers are clear, the implementation is usually the easier part. The important decision has already been made: how much of the product are we willing to lose when one dependency fails?
