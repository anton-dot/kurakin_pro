Backward Compatibility: New Code Must Survive Old Clients

The server has been updated, but the mobile app has not. How do you change a contract without forcing every client to migrate tonight?

The company has deployed a new server. Some users have updated their mobile app; others have not. A partner integration follows its own schedule. Asking everyone to upgrade on the same night is easy on a slide and much harder in a real business.

Backward compatibility means the new system can still serve clients that rely on the previous contract. It reduces the risk of independent releases, provided the team knows which behaviour was actually promised.

Adding a field can be safer than changing a meaning

An additional response field is often harmless to a client that ignores unknown properties. But strict clients may reject even that change. Removing a field, changing its type or introducing a new required parameter is usually more dangerous.

A silent change in meaning can be worse. Suppose “confirmed” used to mean an order was accepted, and now means payment cleared. The schema can remain identical while the business flow changes significantly.

Compatibility is therefore more than JSON or a versioned URL. It includes errors, lifecycle expectations and the business promises that consumers inferred from old behaviour.

Let the provider and consumer deploy separately

In an incremental migration, the provider first supports both forms or introduces a new optional path. Consumers then adopt it at their own pace. The old behaviour disappears only after there is evidence that nobody still depends on it.

A database change may use Expand–Contract. An API may support old and new representations, sometimes with explicit versioning. Pick the technique for the dependency boundary, not because a pattern name looks appealing.

Verify compatibility rather than asserting it

Know which consumer versions are running and which interactions matter to them. Contract tests can reveal specific incompatibilities before deployment. Usage measurements show who is still calling an older path.

But an outwardly compatible response can still contain the wrong business meaning. Changes to refund eligibility or discount rules need product-level validation, even when the API shape remains stable.

The cost of letting clients update later

A migration window means additional fixes, tests, support and sometimes restrictions. It should not last forever. Partners need a clear statement of when old behaviour will stop being supported and a realistic route to the new contract.

Backward compatibility is not a promise never to break anything. It gives a business room to change different parts of a system at different speeds, without one collective cutover night.
