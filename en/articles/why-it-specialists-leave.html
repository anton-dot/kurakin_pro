Why IT Specialists Change Jobs

IT professionals often tolerate dissatisfaction until the last moment. By the time a resignation reaches the manager’s desk, the decision may already be final—even though many departures could have been prevented.

Translation Original Russian article on iCareer ↗

IT specialists are creative people, and employers need to work with them proactively. They may remain silent and tolerate a problem for a long time. Everything may appear satisfactory one evening, yet the next morning the employee is standing beside the manager’s desk with a resignation letter.

Finding strong IT specialists is difficult and time-consuming, with much of the burden falling on HR. It is particularly frustrating when someone leaves after only a short period and the exit interview later reveals that the loss could have been avoided.

The reasons below are among the most common factors that lead IT specialists to leave a company.

Main Reasons

Money

There is a persistent belief that money is not a strong motivator. If that were completely true, however, many people would not go to work at all, and banks and gold mines would never be attacked.

Even employees driven by ideas and enthusiasm experience frustration when colleagues with comparable experience earn one and a half or two times more.

What employers can do

One alternative to maintaining a large internal IT team is to use cloud-based, off-the-shelf information systems, especially products built on low-code technology. The initial costs may appear disproportionate, but they can pay for themselves over several years.

Project bonuses or payments linked to company performance can provide another tool. Even when a business cannot match the market salary, periodic additional compensation helps maintain interest.

This model benefits both the company and the employee, but it works only when the fixed salary covers normal monthly expenses and supports a reasonable quality of life.

The most important part of financial motivation is honoring agreements—and revisiting old agreements when circumstances require it.

Permanent Maintenance Mode

IT specialists usually enjoy creating and developing new systems. Sooner or later, however, even a modern system enters maintenance mode and eventually reaches the disposal phase. That period can last for years, and someone still has to support the product.

During interviews, employers often promise that the company will eventually move to modern technology, develop a new system, adopt a more current stack, and stop forcing employees to work indefinitely with legacy platforms. Then the promises are not fulfilled.

Paradoxically, the more successfully employees keep old systems alive, the less willing the organization becomes to replace them.

As systems become outdated, employees’ skills may become outdated as well. One day, a specialist looks up, studies the market, and decides to leave.

IT professionals are often driven by change. After a certain income level, development and novelty can matter more than additional money. Boredom is one of the strongest demotivators.

What employers can do

Employers need to invest in the evolution of their systems. When continued internal development is impractical, the company can migrate to more modern products and technology stacks.

The encouraging news is that an increasing number of technological solutions are available free of charge.

Companies should also expand employees’ skills through conferences, meetups, hackathons, and similar activities. Internal conferences are especially useful because specialists can exchange knowledge and raise the overall capability of the team.

Other options include allowing experiments with emerging technologies, supporting employees’ personal projects, and encouraging participation in open-source communities.

The employee gains professional development and self-realization. The company retains a stronger specialist and improves its employer brand.

Managers

Two qualities of a manager have a major influence on how long an IT specialist remains with a company: professional competence and management style.

A manager with a weak understanding of IT can become a serious source of irritation for experienced professionals. Less principled experts may also exploit that lack of competence for their own benefit.

An excessively authoritarian style suppresses creativity and limits professional growth. A style that is too democratic can produce the opposite problem: loss of focus and failure to reach goals.

What employers can do

Ideally, an IT team should be managed by someone with a background in IT.

When a company employs only a small number of technical specialists, modern task-management systems can help organize work. Even then, the manager cannot avoid understanding what the IT employees actually do.

Today, a manager needs not only legal, financial, and management competence but also a practical understanding of information technology.

Secondary Reasons

Bureaucracy and Reporting

Most IT specialists dislike bureaucracy, but they still understand the value of documenting functions, architecture, data fields, and infrastructure.

Documentation helps new employees join a project more quickly. It also saves experienced specialists from spending hours reconstructing their own reasoning when they return to a task six months later.

The problem begins when a five-minute change involving only a few lines requires approval from a long chain of managers.

Meaningless or Unused Work

IT specialists need to understand why they are doing something and who will use the result. When they cannot see the effect of their work, motivation and engagement decline on future projects.

Employees should understand that they are building a cathedral rather than merely laying bricks. The company strategy therefore needs to become a guide for action, not another document hidden on the corporate website.

Excessive Pressure and Overtime

The main cost of overtime is employee burnout. Valuable specialists who recently worked seven days a week to rescue one project may then spend several weeks unable to regain momentum on the next.

Overtime can be justified when the team must urgently correct a serious defect, recover a schedule, or respond to force majeure. It should not become the normal operating model.

A manager’s responsibility is to protect and preserve the team as a strategic resource. That means ensuring that employees maintain a healthy pattern of work and rest.

When excessive pressure comes from senior leadership, the manager should act as a shield and protect the team from destructive influence.

The patience and silence common among IT specialists can create an illusion that pressure and overtime are effective. That illusion encourages managers to push even harder—until the employee leaves.

Catalysts

Catalysts are not necessarily the original cause of a job change, but they accelerate the decision. They interrupt the routine and encourage an employee to look up from daily tasks and assess the company and the wider labor market.

  • A change of management. In practice, this may mean changes in strategy, team composition, management systems, the technology stack, and cost-cutting priorities.
  • A change in family circumstances. A new family member may influence the employee’s decisions or create additional expenses.
  • An office move. The disruption creates a natural pause in which an employee can reconsider their position in the company and on the market.
  • A promotion. Not every specialist wants to become a manager, but some are afraid to say so because they may be considered unambitious or passive.
  • A budget reduction. Cutting the budget usually means reducing costs, and employees may reasonably expect investment in new technology to be among the first things removed.

Most catalysts are foreseeable events. Companies can prepare employees by explaining why a change is necessary and what consequences to expect. Transparency reduces tension and anxiety.

Conclusion

Money is a serious motivator for IT specialists, and many companies genuinely cannot afford to pay more. Compensation, however, is only one part of retention.

Employers can still act on many of the other reasons people leave. They can create a more comfortable environment, support development, reduce unnecessary bureaucracy, protect employees from chronic overload, and give technical work a visible connection to the company’s purpose.

IT specialists are a kind of litmus test. They are often among the first employees to react when a company begins to stagnate.

In the past, opening new areas and constantly experimenting may have seemed like the greater organizational risk. Today, conservatism itself can be the greater risk.
