Data Warehouse: Why Analytics Should Not Compete With the Production System

Labour, parts and site visits live in different systems. The cost report looks complete but counts some expenses twice. How do we make the number traceable?

A service director wants the true cost of a field repair. Work orders live in one system, spare parts in another and technicians' time in a third. Analysts join the tables and get a total. The problem appears later: a job with two visits and three material lines has multiplied some of its costs.

A data warehouse can help, but merely creating a separate database will not repair a wrong number. It gives the company a place to combine and verify information without forcing production systems to serve heavy reports. People still have to agree on what counts as a cost of a job.

Decide what one row represents

Joining tables by work-order ID is not enough. Labour, parts and visits exist at different levels of detail. One record may represent a single engineer visit, another a spare-part issue and another the complete customer job. A join between two one-to-many sets can multiply amounts even though the database has done exactly what it was asked.

Start by declaring the grain of each fact — what a single row means. The Kimball Group's dimensional modelling guidance makes this the foundation of reliable fact tables. Visit cost, material-line cost and job-level totals may need separate structures before they are safely aggregated by business identity.

Even a cancelled visit needs a decision. It may have produced no customer outcome yet still incurred travel and labour costs. Assigning zero cost or removing it from the report is a business rule, not a SQL performance setting.

Whose definition of cost is correct?

Finance may allocate labour using accounting rates. Operations may care about actual engineer hours. Regional management may want transport included. All three views can be useful, but it is misleading to publish them under one metric name without saying what they contain.

A published measure needs a definition, an accountable owner and a clear inclusion rule. Its date basis matters too. Does an amount belong to the month of service, the month it was posted, or the date it entered analytics? Those are different questions and can produce different monthly totals.

Comparing regions requires agreed definitions of sites, customers, materials and people. A shared ID alone may not resolve history. A work order could move between branches, so the report must decide whether to attribute its cost to the branch performing the work then or the branch currently owning the account.

Keep analytical load away from operational work

Production databases are tuned for creating and updating orders and checking current state. A year-long report may scan, aggregate and join large volumes. Without workload isolation, those queries can compete with transactions customers are waiting for.

A warehouse provides a separate place for extraction, transformations, history and analytical queries. But isolation has to be real. If every warehouse load holds locks or overloads the source, the conflict has merely moved to ingestion. Choose an appropriate extraction method, such as scheduled extracts or change capture, and measure its impact.

Not every application requires a fully separate warehouse platform. A read replica, prepared reporting tables or a materialised view may be simpler for a small workload. A dedicated layer becomes easier to justify when multiple sources, queries and teams repeatedly pay to transform the same information.

Yesterday's transaction can change today

Suppose a spare part was charged to a job at month-end. A week later, accounting discovers the assignment was wrong and moves the cost. Analytics must not only ingest the correction but also change the earlier result. Returns, cancellations and labour-time corrections create similar problems.

Loading only records with new creation timestamps will miss some of these updates. Reliable pipelines need business keys, update and deletion handling, duplicate protection and a way to recalculate affected periods. For audit work, the earlier value and the correction may both matter.

Every report also needs a data-as-of time and visibility into late sources. A dashboard labelled “today” may combine fresh work orders with yesterday's material postings. Instant consistency with the operational transaction is possible only if explicitly designed and paid for; it is not a standard property of warehousing.

Trace the number back to the work

Take a few normal and difficult jobs: a return visit, a moved part, a cancellation and work completed across month-end. Reconstruct their costs from source records. Compare them with the warehouse and determine whether differences follow an agreed rule or an accidental join.

Then inspect the broader reconciliation. Track not only loaded row counts but missing keys, duplicates, source lag and changes to historical periods. If a total stops matching, the responsible team should be able to find the specific work and replay the transformation without manually rewriting an unexplained figure.

A warehouse pays off not merely when a dashboard opens quickly, but when a manager can use its number and, if challenged, follow it back to the actual business operations that produced it.
