Women in Leadership: Business Has Confused Toughness with Strength for Too Long

Qualities once dismissed as soft are becoming practical management tools for complex modern companies.

When people talk about a strong leader, it is still easy to imagine someone who makes decisions quickly, defends their position firmly, demands results, and rarely shows doubt.

But perhaps our idea of what makes a leader strong is becoming outdated.

Over the years, I have worked with many leaders, and I keep noticing certain qualities that often stand out particularly clearly in women. Not in every woman. And certainly not only in women. But often enough to be worth paying attention to.

Listening Does Not Mean Being Soft

One of those qualities is the ability to listen.

Not simply waiting for your turn to speak. Not running a meeting just to confirm a decision that has already been made. But actually collecting different perspectives before reaching a conclusion.

In a more traditional management culture, this can sometimes be mistaken for indecision.

I think the opposite is true.

Any leader can make a decision in five minutes if they are not interested in what the team thinks. Listening to ten different perspectives, separating emotion from facts, and then taking responsibility for the final decision is much harder.

Relationships Are a Management Tool Too

Tom Peters has been provocatively writing about the strengths of women in leadership for years. One idea that resonates with me is that women often lead through involvement and collaboration rather than hierarchy alone.

And there is an important distinction here.

Relationships inside a team are not corporate therapy, and they are not about trying to make everyone happy.

They are about understanding who really influences the outcome, where conflict is starting to emerge, who has stopped contributing in meetings, why a strong employee has suddenly lost initiative, and where a process that still looks fine on paper has already started to break down.

You can call this empathy.

I would call it management awareness.

You Do Not Have to Become an “Iron Lady”

There is another paradox.

When a woman enters a traditionally male management environment, she may sometimes feel that she has to prove she can be even tougher than the men around her.

She probably can.

But why should she?

If your strength is the ability to build trust, notice more nuance, bring people together, and still deliver results, why abandon that just to fit someone else’s definition of a strong leader?

A strong leader does not have to be the loudest person in the room.

And confidence does not have to look like certainty.

Perhaps the Future Favors This Kind of Leadership

Modern companies are becoming more complex. Fewer and fewer problems can be solved by simply giving an order from the top.

Leaders have to align different functions. Retain strong people. Operate in uncertainty. Admit that they do not have all the answers. Build an environment where people are willing to disagree and still continue working effectively together.

And qualities that were once dismissively described as “feminine” or “soft” suddenly turn out to be highly practical.

The ability to listen.

The ability to build relationships.

Attention to people.

A willingness to discuss rather than simply instruct.

The ability to be firm without constantly demonstrating toughness.

Perhaps this is not an alternative management style.

Perhaps it is simply good modern leadership.

And perhaps women developed some of these capabilities earlier.

This is where the discussion becomes interesting.

Is there really such a thing as a female leadership style? Or have we simply started to value leadership qualities that were once mistakenly considered not “strong” enough?
