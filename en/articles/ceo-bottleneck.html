How a CEO Becomes the Company's Bottleneck

Sometimes a company is slow not because employees perform poorly, but because too many decisions are waiting for one person — the CEO.

At an early stage, this can be useful. The founder knows the product, customers, and context better than anyone, so routing decisions through them can be fast.

The problem begins when the company grows but the decision model does not.

Every important question moves upward

Pricing, hiring, product details, partnerships, disputes between teams — everything waits for the CEO's final word.

Each decision may take only a few minutes. A queue of hundreds of decisions determines the speed of the company.

The team learns to wait

If independent decisions are regularly reversed from above, the rational response is to stop taking the risk.

Eventually the CEO complains about lack of initiative in a system they personally trained to wait for approval.

Information gets filtered

When everything must pass through one person, context is inevitably compressed. Managers bring simplified options upward and important details disappear.

The CEO makes more decisions while knowing less about each one.

Delegate decision rights, not only tasks

Scale appears when leaders have clear areas where prior approval is not required.

The CEO still owns direction, standards, and critical exceptions, but stops being a mandatory participant in every step.

The CEO's calendar should not define business speed

If a project moves only after a meeting with the CEO, the problem is no longer the project.

A company cannot scale faster than its narrowest bottleneck. Sometimes that bottleneck is the person who wants growth more than anyone else.
