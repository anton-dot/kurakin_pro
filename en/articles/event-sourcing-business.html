Event Sourcing: When the History Matters More Than the Latest Row

The order total is correct, but nobody can explain how it got there. When keeping business events as the source of truth is worth the long-term cost.

An order total has changed. The current value is correct, but months later a manager asks why that particular discount was applied. If every update simply overwrote the previous state, the system may no longer contain enough information to answer.

Event Sourcing makes the history of business facts the primary record and derives current state from it. That can be valuable when the path to an outcome matters as much as the outcome itself. It also requires treating history as a long-lived data model, not as a convenient log file.

State is the result; events are the evidence

A conventional order row may store today's total and status. With Event Sourcing, the primary record is an ordered sequence of events: order created, item added, discount applied, payment confirmed. The current state is rebuilt by applying those events.

That does not mean a customer screen should replay the entire history for every request. Applications usually maintain read projections and may store snapshots to speed up recovery. The events, however, remain the source of truth.

Do not confuse this with event-driven architecture. A service can publish events to other systems while storing its own current state normally. Event Sourcing can also be implemented within one application without an extensive inter-service event platform.

Questions the history can answer

A well-designed event history lets the team reconstruct how an entity changed and build new views from past facts. It might reveal when an order reached its final price or support recalculating a report with a different projection.

But it is not a mind-reading audit system. “Discount applied” does not explain who approved an exception or why unless that information was recorded at the time. The usefulness of history depends on the quality of the events.

Corrections usually become new events rather than edits that silently rewrite the past. The sequence has to keep a meaningful relationship between cause and effect.

Tomorrow's code must understand yesterday's events

Event schemas change. Older orders may lack fields introduced later, and the meaning of a status may evolve. Rebuilding state requires either maintaining support for historical event versions or carefully migrating them.

Replay must also be separated from external side effects. Rebuilding a projection should not charge customers again or resend years of notifications. Append-only storage does not make that safe automatically.

Personal data creates another difficult boundary. Keeping names, addresses or sensitive details inside permanent events can complicate correction, retention and deletion requirements. Access controls and a data-lifecycle design need to be part of the initial decision.

An audit log may be enough

Sometimes the business only needs a record of who changed an important value and when. Audit logs or row versioning can solve that without making every entity an event stream.

I would choose Event Sourcing only when the history of business facts is central to the product, to investigations, or to rebuilding different views — and the team can afford to maintain that history through future schema changes.

Event Sourcing is justified when the past is part of the product. If someone merely wants to check the past now and then, a simpler audit trail is often the better investment.
