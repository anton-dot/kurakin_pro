Optimistic vs Pessimistic Locking: Choosing Between Conflict and Waiting

When several operations change the same data at the same time, the system must choose whether to lock early or allow concurrency and resolve conflicts later.

What Problem Are We Solving?

Without concurrency control, two users or processes can read the same state, modify it independently, and silently overwrite each other. There is no universal solution: strong locking prevents conflict but makes others wait, while optimistic control preserves concurrency but requires conflict detection.

How It Works

Pessimistic locking reserves the resource until the operation finishes. Optimistic locking avoids holding a long lock and checks a version or similar marker when writing. If the data changed, the update fails and needs retry or a new decision. The choice must consider UX because automatic retry is not safe for every action.

What the Business Gets

The business gets a deliberate balance between throughput and strong protection of critical updates. Rare conflicts do not have to pay for permanent serialization, while frequent high-cost collisions may justify stronger coordination.

What the Team Gets

Teams get an explicit concurrency strategy instead of accidental lost updates. Conflict counts, lock wait, and retries become observable signals showing when the original choice no longer matches real traffic.

What the Customer Gets

Customers get either controlled waiting or a clear message that data changed and the action must be reconsidered, rather than silent loss of work.

What We Pay For It

Pessimistic locking reduces concurrency and introduces wait time and deadlock risk. Optimistic locking moves complexity into conflict handling and UX. Under growth a poor choice becomes either a queue or a retry storm.

When Not to Add It

When concurrent updates are rare, optimistic control often preserves capacity more efficiently. When the same entity is frequently changed at once and conflict is unacceptable, stronger locking may be justified.

What to Ask Before the Decision

  • How often do real conflicts happen?
  • What costs more: waiting or retrying?
  • Can the operation be retried safely?
  • How does the user learn that the data changed?
  • Do we monitor lock wait, deadlocks, and optimistic conflicts?

In the End

Locking decides where to pay for concurrency: upfront through waiting or later through conflict handling. The right strategy protects business outcomes without buying more serialization than the process actually needs.
