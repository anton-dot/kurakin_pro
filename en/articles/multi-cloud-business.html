Multi-Cloud: A Second Provider Is Not Yet a Backup Plan

Two cloud providers look safer than one. But who moves customers during an outage, where is the data, and what does keeping the backup ready cost?

The proposal sounds reassuring: deploy to two cloud providers and remove the risk of depending on one. On a slide, it becomes two identical platforms joined by an arrow. In production, it means different networks, identity systems, managed services, invoices and operational skills.

Before funding that architecture, I would ask which specific event the second cloud is meant to protect against. Without a clear answer, we may be buying extra complexity rather than resilience.

Which failure are we buying protection from?

Multi-cloud can describe very different arrangements: one product on one provider and another product elsewhere; off-provider backups; a disaster-recovery site; or an application serving traffic across two providers at once.

There is also the commercial reason. Changing contract terms or an unplanned price increase can make dependence uncomfortable. That is a legitimate concern, but it does not automatically call for a fully operational duplicate platform around the clock.

Loss of one availability zone, a regional outage, a failure affecting an entire provider and a planned migration over several months are not interchangeable scenarios. Treating them all as “resilience” hides the decision the business actually needs to make.

Disaster recovery is not the same as an exit strategy

For a disruption, two terms make the discussion concrete. RTO is how long the business can tolerate before the service is restored. RPO is how much recent data it can afford to lose if not every change has reached the recovery environment.

An application might boot on another provider quickly while the last usable copy of its data is far too old. Or the data may be current, but routing, identity, certificates, secrets and manual changes take hours to make work. Saying “we have two clouds” tells us little about either result.

An exit plan addresses a different problem. It defines how to move data, replace provider-specific components and migrate deliberately when business circumstances require it. For many organisations, that is more sensible than keeping a permanently hot copy of everything.

One provider with two regions might be enough

If the concern is a zone or regional outage, start by evaluating disaster-recovery options within the existing provider. Separate regions have their own limitations, but staying with familiar tooling can simplify security, operations and recovery procedures.

A second provider does not remove shared dependencies. DNS, identity, corporate networking, a payment processor or a faulty release can still take down both deployments. True independence has to be evaluated along the whole customer journey, not by counting vendor logos.

Backups, tested restoration and a workable switchover process can deliver more value than a sophisticated multi-cloud environment nobody has exercised since launch. Google Cloud's business-continuity patterns make a similar distinction between single-provider multi-region recovery and cross-provider disaster recovery.

The price is not only the second infrastructure bill

Each cloud has its own identity rules, networking, storage, observability and operational behaviour. A genuine fallback requires people who can diagnose and restore service on either platform. Those skills and exercises cost time even when everything is healthy.

Replicating data across providers introduces transfer charges and consistency decisions. Synchronous replication can add latency and tight coupling. Asynchronous replication can leave a window of recent changes that have not reached the standby environment. Neither removes risk altogether.

There is also a temptation to avoid useful managed services so the application looks identical everywhere. Sometimes portability is worth that trade-off. Sometimes the company ends up operating databases and queues itself to protect against a hypothetical migration, spending far more than it saves.

Show me the recovery, not another architecture diagram

Before approving the larger operating budget, I would want a controlled recovery exercise. Remove the primary site and see what happens to an actual customer flow: sign-in, placing an order, charging a payment, reading existing data. Record recovery time, data loss and manual steps. Then test the way back, including reconciliation of any changes made during the outage.

If the team cannot run the exercise, that tells us something important. We have a recovery design, but we have not demonstrated recovery within the promised time.

Put a price on that alternative path for an ordinary month: infrastructure, transfer, licences, security, team effort and regular exercises. Compare it with the consequences of the specific threat being addressed. Do not compare it with zero, because a single-cloud system still needs backups and sound reliability engineering.

When a second provider earns its keep

Sometimes regulation, data-location constraints, regional availability or the impact of provider-wide failure make the investment justified. Different providers may also offer distinct capabilities that suit different workloads without requiring the entire application to operate in both places.

A company that must survive a complete provider outage may reasonably pay for well-tested multi-cloud recovery. Another may get a better outcome from solid multi-region design, reliable restores and an exit plan that has been rehearsed.

The second cloud becomes insurance only when someone has proved it protects against the named risk. Until then, it is simply a second environment, and the bills have already started.
