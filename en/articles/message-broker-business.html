Message Broker: Why Separate the Sender of an Event From Its Consumers

When one system needs to tell another that something happened, a direct call looks simplest. As consumers, traffic spikes, and reliability requirements grow, that simplicity becomes coupling.

Imagine that after an order is placed, the company needs to update inventory, send a notification, feed analytics, and trigger several more processes. The obvious approach is to call every system directly.

That works while the number of consumers is small. Over time, the sender starts knowing too much about who must process the event and how.

What problem are we solving?

A message broker — a queue or messaging platform — sits in the middle. The sender publishes a message or event, and consumers process it independently.

Specific technologies differ. Architecturally, the key point is that the sender no longer has to wait for every consumer or know their internal details.

What does the business get?

The main benefit is lower cost of adding new reactions to existing events. If a new process must start after an order tomorrow, the order service does not always need to change.

A broker can also absorb bursts. If a consumer cannot keep up temporarily, messages can wait instead of forcing the entire flow to fail immediately.

This lets different parts of the business scale at different speeds instead of forcing every process to run synchronously.

What does the team get?

Teams get looser coupling and a standard way to build asynchronous workflows. But they also inherit new work: retries, ordering, deduplication, dead letters, schema evolution, and queue observability.

What does the customer get?

The core customer flow can become more resilient. A temporary analytics problem, for example, does not need to block checkout.

The trade-off is that some results may appear later. The product must be clear when a status is still propagating through the system.

What do we pay for it?

The broker becomes another critical infrastructure component that must be operated, scaled, monitored, and recovered.

The flow of data is also harder to understand. With direct calls, service A calls service B. With messaging, an event may pass through several consumers and retries.

The company trades application coupling for asynchronous operational complexity.

When is a broker unnecessary?

If the interaction is simple, synchronous, and the result is needed immediately, a normal API is often better. Especially when there are few systems and traffic is predictable.

Do not add a queue just because the architecture is supposed to be “event-driven.”

What should we ask before deciding?

  • Does the sender need the result immediately?
  • How many independent consumers will the event have?
  • What happens if one consumer is unavailable?
  • Can a message be delivered more than once?
  • Who owns event schemas and compatibility?

In the end

A message broker is useful not because queues are more modern than direct calls, but because the business truly needs independence between producers and consumers.

We gain flexibility and resilience, but pay with behavior that is harder to see and must be deliberately controlled.
