Why Strong Engineers Do Not Always Become Good Managers

Promoting the best engineer feels natural. But management requires almost the opposite habit: stop being the main problem solver and learn to make other people stronger.

One of the most common ways to choose a new manager is to promote the strongest specialist on the team.

The logic is understandable: the person knows the system, has credibility, and solves difficult problems. The problem is that the new role creates value in a different way.

Value used to come from personal expertise

A strong engineer is used to difficult problems being solved through their own knowledge. That is a useful habit for an expert and a dangerous one for a manager.

A manager succeeds not when they solve the hardest problem personally, but when the team can solve hard problems without constant intervention.

Doing it yourself really is faster

A new manager often can complete the task faster than the employee. That makes taking the work back very tempting.

It helps in the short term. In the long term, the team learns to escalate difficult work upward and the manager becomes the busiest person in the room.

Expertise easily turns into control

The better a manager knows the subject, the harder it can be to accept a decision different from their own.

If every discussion ends with “I would do it differently,” people stop proposing alternatives and start guessing the manager's preference.

Feedback becomes part of the job

Code gives relatively quick feedback: it works or it does not. People are more complicated.

Managers must discuss expectations, conflict, development, weak performance, and motivation. These conversations may be less comfortable than technical work, but they become core responsibilities.

You have to live without full control

A manager rarely has all the information. People make dozens of decisions without them, and that is normal.

The manager's job is to create clear boundaries and context, not to be present in every decision.

Career growth should not mean management only

Part of the problem begins before the promotion. If the only way to gain status and compensation is to become a manager, the company pushes strong specialists into roles they may not want.

A strong expert career path matters just as much as a management path.

What helps the transition

New managers need to hear that their results will now be measured differently. They need support with one-on-ones, feedback, delegation, and conflict — and permission not to know the answer first.

The best engineer can become an excellent manager, but only if they stop proving their value in the same way they did as an engineer.
